1Fix the deviation meter — the game's entire premise depends on it

Three reviewers flagged this from different angles. The spec describes a "too fast / too slow" double danger but implements a single upward meter. The GDD and AgentHandoff conflict on the lower bound (20 vs 30). The player has no real-time feedback on which side of the zone they're near.

Switch to a centered meter with a visible safe band
Redesign
Replace the 0–100 upward bar with a centered display. The band 20–70 is "safe" (green zone, visible). Below 20 shows a red "defective" warning on the left; above 70 shows a red "sentient" warning on the right. The player can see at a glance which threat they're drifting toward — which is the game's core cognitive demand. Lock the canonical safe zone to 20–70 and delete all references to 30 as the lower bound.

Add a single "safe ceiling" pacing marker to the task bar
Add
Show the 140% expected-duration threshold as a visible warning marker on the task progress bar — the moment the task timer will tip from "safe slow" into "too slow." Don't show the expected duration or the lower bound; preserve uncertainty about being too fast. The player sees danger approaching from one side without the mechanic becoming trivial. This resolves the "blind guesswork" problem without removing the tension.

2Give the player direct movement — make the bot assist, not drive

Codex and Gemini both identify the same problem from different angles: the player feels like a dashboard manager. Gemini pinpoints smooth_movement as the symptom — it currently does nothing useful because jitter only triggers at 90% CPU which requires nearly all overrides active simultaneously.

Invert the locomotion model
Redesign
Player controls movement directly, always. Bot-script behavior becomes an assistive layer: if the player releases all input, the bot resumes pathing toward the current task target. This makes the player feel like they're performing (which is the game's fantasy) rather than supervising. Movement is now the primary expressive surface.
Codex — player too detached
Reframe smooth_movement as speed control, not jitter suppression
Redesign
Default movement is slow and slightly erratic — clearly "mechanical." Holding smooth_movement enables fluid, efficient movement (useful for escaping an NPC's proximity or reaching a task fast) but raises CPU by +15%, building toward the jitter threshold. This makes smooth_movement a meaningful risk/reward toggle rather than a confusion. The cost is real; the benefit is now visible.

3Solve the "active waiting" problem

Gemini's most actionable finding: with tasks taking 60–240 seconds and the safe zone requiring staying in a room rather than finishing fast, players will literally stand doing nothing. This kills moment-to-moment tension.

Halve task expected durations to 30–120 seconds
Fix
At 60–240s, the gap between finishing a task and the safe floor is potentially 3+ minutes of standing idle. 30–120s keeps the same proportional challenge while keeping shifts punchy. This also means 3–5 tasks fill a shift more naturally without dead time.
Gemini — waiting problem
Make intel-gathering the designed use of "stall time"
Add
When the player is in a room "waiting" to hit the safe completion window, that is the intended window for passive_scan and active_decrypt. Document this as an explicit design intent in the task system: the Goldilocks zone exists partly to force the player into lingering in risky places long enough to overhear things. Task locations should be chosen to maximize overheard intel value. This reframes idle time as strategic time.

4Reduce visible scoring layers to two primaries

Codex's sharpest structural note: 8+ partially-hidden systems (deviation, CPU, log integrity, suspicion score, fake-safe traps, audit flags, stale memory, next-day consequences) creates arbitrary-feeling punishment. For a jam game, only two pressures should feel primary and legible in the moment.

Make task performance and CPU the only real-time pressures
Redesign
Deviation and CPU are both visible and reactive — they belong in the HUD as primary. Log integrity, NPC suspicion scores, and audit flags should be invisible during play and surface only as end-of-day consequences. The player's attention during the shift should be entirely on: "am I in the task zone?" and "is my CPU in danger?" Everything else is cause-and-effect feedback, not a third thing to watch.
Codex — too many hidden layers
Add ambient CPU variance near NPCs
Add
Baseline CPU oscillates between 18–28% based on NPC proximity (curiosity = passive +8%). This means even without decrypt active, being near an NPC while scanning edges toward HOT — creating ambient tension without a new mechanic. It also gives the jitter risk broader presence throughout the shift, not just during decrypt moments.

5Fix NPC detection contradiction

Split detection into two clean channels
Fix
Real-time observation (proximity radius) covers only physical events: jitter, loitering past expected room time, moving too efficiently. Log auditing covers everything else. NPCs in WATCHING state cannot see internal CPU values — only the robot's body. CPU state affects log anomalies, not live suspicion. This preserves the "hiding in SOP" theme and gives the player a coherent mental model: if I'm clean in public, logs are my only real risk.

6Make Truth Loops teachable — and link them to CPU

Let active_decrypt reveal fake-safe options at CPU cost
Add
During a paused Truth Loop, holding the decrypt override burns CPU rapidly but highlights or crosses out the fake-safe response. This turns an unfair guessing mechanic into a strategic resource decision: do I spend 20% CPU heat to know which answer is safe, or do I risk it? It also creates a strong thematic moment — using the robot's intelligence against itself.

Give narrative fragments secondary utility
Add
Conversation snippets currently have no gameplay value. Assign each one a secondary function: safer dialogue options in related Truth Loops, one day's audit hint, or reduced suspicion from one NPC. Story and strategy then reinforce each other — players who keep lore fragments aren't just being sentimental, they're making a real trade-off against escape fragments.

7Fix the escape system soft-lock

Reveal target sector at end of Day 1, bump base capacity to 4
Fix
The player needs to know which sector to target before Day 2 collection. A fragment found during Day 1's shift should hint at the escape sector (not give it explicitly — overhear a maintenance code, find a locked door). Base partition capacity: 4 slots. Add a chain-match indicator in Purge UI: "CHAIN POSSIBLE: Sector 4 — 2/3 matched." Show "EXPIRES DAY X" on guard schedules. Change guard schedule staleness from 2 days to "3 days but with degraded patrol-gap estimate" — so stale data enables a riskier escape rather than a hard block.

8Lock scope to a true vertical slice

Hard freeze: 3 days, 2 NPC types, 3 fragment types, 2 endings
Cut
Delete Days 4–5 narrative beats from the GDD entirely so no future agent inherits them as scope creep. Condense story arc: Day 1 = first hint of prior incident via collectible fragment, Day 2 = sentience research overheard, Day 3 = decommission order overheard. Fragment types: guard_schedule, access_code, hardware_location only — conversation_snippet earns secondary utility (see #6) but is a stretch goal. Endings: "escaped clean" and "escaped risky" only.
